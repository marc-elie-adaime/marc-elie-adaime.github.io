Recovering evolutionary and ecological history from incomplete records

My research combines computational methods (including phylogenetics and image analysis), evolutionary theory, and paleoecology to reconstruct biodiversity dynamics, trait evolution, and environmental change from the incomplete evidence organisms leave behind, from molecular sequences to the morphology of fossilized plants.

Training & research questions

  • 2017 – 2019 · Université Laval, Québec

    M.Sc. Quaternary paleoecology in the Canadian High Arctic

    Laboratoire de paléoécologie aquatique · Centre d'études nordiques · Département de géographie

    I began in physical geography and Quaternary science, reconstructing postglacial climate and landscape change from the sediments of Canada's northernmost lakes. My M.Sc. produced the first deglaciation chronology for the Fury and Hecla Strait region of Nunavut, along with evidence for a postglacial marine reconnection between Atlantic and Pacific currents. This work drew on diatoms, marine and freshwater microalgae, together with geochemical proxies.

  • 2019 – 2024 · University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign

    Ph.D. Deep learning for pollen morphology

    Punyasena Lab · Department of Plant Biology · School of Integrative Biology

    My doctoral research developed evolutionarily aware neural networks that extract phylogenetically and ecologically meaningful information from pollen morphology. Using superresolution imaging and deep learning, I built approaches that bridge molecular phylogenetics and the analysis of morphological data. These made it possible to place unknown plant morphotypes into reference molecular phylogenies from morphology alone, to detect adaptations that arose in plant populations over evolutionary time, and to reconstruct changes in grassland diversity and photosynthetic composition across tens of thousands of years.

  • 2025 – present · Smithsonian Institution

    Postdoctoral Research Fellow

    Chief Data and AI Office, Office of Digital & Innovation · Washington, D.C.

    I now work at the interface of ecology, evolution, and biodiversity informatics. A central question drives much of this: why are conifers so much more widespread in North America than in South America, under what conditions do South American conifers succeed beyond expectation, and do trait distributions across environmental zones reflect deterministic environmental filtering or stochastic processes? To address this, I develop new ways to extract anatomical, physiological, and genomic traits from a range of organisms, with a particular focus on conifers, and integrate them with forest-plot data to model trait-environment relationships at scale on high-performance computing infrastructure housed at the Smithsonian.

  • From January 2027 · American University of Beirut

    Assistant Professor, Bioinformatics & Computational Biology

    Department of Biology · Faculty of Arts and Sciences

    An incoming faculty position where I will build a research program extending many of these methods to the biodiversity of Lebanon and the eastern Mediterranean. I aim to understand why endemic and regional species occur under particular environmental conditions, how and why their traits emerged over evolutionary history, and how present-day and extinct lineages are related. This work also aims to advance new methods for phylogenetic estimation, such as inferring phylogenies and quantifying phylogenetic uncertainty, alongside approaches that bridge image analysis and bioinformatics. Together, they combine molecular and phenotypic data from living and extinct organisms to reconstruct the past environments that shaped these ecosystems and the life-forms within them.

Evolution and adaptation across deep time

I use molecular phylogenetics, evolutionary simulation, and trait analysis to ask why lineages carry the traits they do. Conifers are a recurring focus: the thickening of pollen walls under higher UV to shield DNA, and the puzzling, repeated evolution of fleshy cones in Podocarpaceae. By combining phylogenetic comparative methods with anatomical, physiological, and genomic data, I test how far a trait is explained by environment and selection versus historical contingency.

Ecological and environmental change

The same question, how ecosystems respond to a shifting climate, runs from the extreme cold of the Canadian High Arctic to the warm tropics of East Africa. In Arctic lakes I reconstruct millennia of change from microfossils, geochemistry, and environmental DNA. In grasslands I read diversity and vegetation shifts from fossil pollen. Across both, small changes in a community can reveal large changes in the ecosystem around it.

New computational methods for hard questions in evolution and ecology

Both types of questions depend on measuring variation that is too subtle to observe. I build statistical and computational methods, including signal processing and deep learning on superresolution imaging, that turn complex shapes such as pollen and cones into interpretable representations. This resolves phylogenetic placement, reveals cryptic speciation and extinction events, and identifies adaptations over evolutionary timescales. The resulting tools are released openly for the community.
