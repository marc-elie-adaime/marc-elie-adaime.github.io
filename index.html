Decoding evolutionary and ecological signal from plant morphology and molecular data.

I develop and apply phylogenetic and machine learning methods to study the deep evolutionary history of major plant clades and the response of ecosystems to climate, from postglacial change across the high Arctic and East African grasslands to the diversification of the world's conifers, working across records that range from entire sediment cores to the architecture of a single pollen grain.

About

My work spans the evolution and adaptation of key lineages across Earth's history and the study of past and present-day species communities responding to environmental change, using a shared set of computational and statistical methods.

I trained first as a Quaternary paleoecologist in Québec, reconstructing postglacial environments from Arctic lake sediments. My doctoral work turned to deep learning, training neural networks to extract phylogenetic and ecological signal from pollen morphology. At the Smithsonian, I now integrate the machine-learning methods we develop with forest-plot occurrence data and molecular and anatomical evidence to ask how plant lineages diversify, how they adapt to particular climatic zones, and why certain morphological traits arise, with much of this work focused on conifers.

Current work

Some questions that drive my current work: how lineages evolve over time, and how ecosystems change in response to environmental shifts, along with the methods that allow us to answer both.

01 / EVOLUTION

Evolution and adaptation across deep time

Why do lineages evolve the traits they do? I use phylogenetics, evolutionary simulation, and trait analysis in plant groups like conifers to trace how form responds to environment, from pollen walls that thicken under higher UV to shield DNA, to the puzzling, repeated evolution of fleshy cones. The goal is to connect a trait to the factors that shape it, from environmental pressures to selection to changes in the genome.

02 / ECOLOGICAL CHANGE

Ecosystems and environmental change

How do ecosystems respond to a changing climate? The same question runs from the extreme cold of the Canadian High Arctic to the warm tropics of East Africa, with different data and hypotheses. Using species identification from eDNA and morphology alongside geochemistry, I reconstruct past biological communities, how their diversity changed over time, and what the climate and environment looked like.

03 / METHODS

New methods needed for testing evolutionary and ecological hypotheses

Both questions depend on detecting signals almost too subtle to see. I develop statistical and computational methods, including signal processing and deep learning, that quantify fine-grained phenotypic variation in structures like pollen and cones. This turns shape into rigorous evidence, resolving phylogenetic placement, revealing speciation and extinction events, and catching the small community shifts that mark major ecosystem change.

From field to computational models

My work runs from field data collection to the analysis of complex biological data, both morphological and molecular, using established techniques alongside the new statistical and computational methods we develop.

Beginning January 2027, I join the American University of Beirut as Assistant Professor of Bioinformatics and Computational Biology. There I'll extend this program to the biodiversity of the eastern Mediterranean, studying the adaptation and evolution of key species and developing new methods for phylogenetic inference and diversity estimation. I will also continue my work on polar and tropical ecosystems through established collaborations.
