About

A path from the Arctic to plant evolution, held together by computation.

My research combines phylogenetics, image analysis, evolutionary theory, and paleoecology. The unifying goal is to read biodiversity dynamics, trait evolution, and environmental change from the incomplete evidence organisms leave behind: sediments, sequences, and the complex shapes of fossilized life-forms, mainly plants.

I trained first as a Quaternary paleoecologist in Québec, reconstructing postglacial environments from Arctic lake sediments. My doctoral work turned to deep learning, training neural networks to extract phylogenetic and ecological signal from the morphology of pollen. Today at the Smithsonian I combine these threads, molecular phylogenetics, morphological data, and machine learning, to study how plant lineages diversify and adapt.

Current directions

Three research threads

Two questions about how life and ecosystems change over time, and the methods that make both possible to answer.

EVOLUTION

Evolution and adaptation across deep time

Why do lineages evolve the traits they do? I use phylogenetics, evolutionary simulation, and trait analysis in plant groups like conifers to trace how form responds to environment, from pollen walls that thicken under higher UV to shield DNA, to the puzzling, repeated evolution of fleshy cones. The goal is to connect a trait to its drivers, from environmental pressures to selection to changes in the genome.

ECOLOGICAL CHANGE

Ecosystems and environmental change

How do ecosystems respond to a changing climate? The same question runs from the extreme cold of the Canadian High Arctic to the warm tropics of East Africa, with different data and hypotheses. Using species identification from eDNA and morphology alongside geochemistry, I reconstruct past biological communities, how their diversity changed over time, and what the climate and environment looked like.

METHODS

The methods that make it possible

Both questions depend on detecting signals almost too subtle to see. I develop statistical and computational methods, including signal processing and deep learning, that quantify fine-grained phenotypic variation in structures like pollen and cones. This turns shape into rigorous evidence, resolving phylogenetic placement, revealing speciation and extinction events, and catching the small community shifts that mark major ecosystem change.

Approach

From field to model

My work runs from collecting data in the field to analyzing complex biological data, both morphological and molecular, using established techniques alongside the new statistical and computational methods we develop.

Beginning January 2027, I join the American University of Beirut as Assistant Professor of Bioinformatics and Computational Biology, extending this program to the biodiversity of Lebanon and the eastern Mediterranean.
