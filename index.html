About

My research combines phylogenetics, image analysis, evolutionary theory, and paleoecology. Its unifying goal is to understand how the diversity of key plant lineages changes through time, to model trait evolution, and to infer the environmental factors that drive major shifts in that diversity, all from the incomplete evidence organisms leave behind: sediments, sequences, and the complex shapes of fossilized life-forms, mainly plants.

I trained first in Quaternary paleoecology in Québec, reconstructing postglacial environments from marine and freshwater diatoms and geochemical proxies in Arctic lake sediments of the Fury and Hecla Strait region, a key area where the Atlantic and Pacific oceans reconnected after deglaciation. My doctoral work then turned to deep learning, training neural networks to extract phylogenetically and ecologically meaningful signal from pollen morphology, enabling tasks such as phylogenetic placement, the identification of extinct lineages, and the detection of subtle adaptations to environmental stress over evolutionary timescales. Now, at the Smithsonian, I combine forest-plot data with machine-learning models that automate the extraction of anatomical traits and genomic properties, working to understand why certain plant lineages succeed in some regions but not others, and how their traits shape their ability to establish in particular climates.

Current research

Three research questions

A few questions that drive my current work: how can we trace the way lineages evolve and adapt over time when phenotypic variation is almost impossible to detect by eye, and how can we quantify shifts in the diversity of ecological communities when species are difficult to tell apart, together with the computational approaches that make both questions possible to answer.

EVOLUTIONARY CHANGE

Evolution and adaptation across deep time

Why do lineages evolve the traits they do? I use phylogenetics, evolutionary simulation, and trait analysis in plant groups like conifers to trace how form responds to environment, from pollen walls that thicken under higher UV to protect DNA, to the puzzling, repeated evolution of fleshy cones. The goal is to connect how traits vary to their drivers, from environmental pressures to selection to changes in the genome.

ECOLOGICAL CHANGE

Ecosystems and environmental change

How do ecosystems respond to a changing climate? The same question runs from the extreme cold of the Canadian High Arctic to the warm tropics of East Africa, with different data and hypotheses. Using species identification from eDNA and morphology alongside geochemistry, I reconstruct past biological communities, how their diversity changed over time, and what the climate and environment that shaped them looked like.

METHODS

New methods for open challenges in ecology & evolution

Both questions depend on detecting signals almost too subtle to see. I develop statistical and computational methods, including signal processing and deep learning, that quantify fine-grained phenotypic variation in structures like pollen and cones. This turns shape and texture into robust evidence, resolving phylogenetic placement, revealing speciation and extinction events, and catching the small community shifts that mark major ecosystem change.

Approach

From field data collection to modeling biological systems

My work runs from collecting data in the field to analyzing complex biological data, both morphological and molecular, using established techniques alongside the new statistical and computational methods we develop.

Beginning January 2027, I join the American University of Beirut as Assistant Professor of Bioinformatics and Computational Biology, extending this program to the biodiversity of Lebanon and the eastern Mediterranean. I will also continue developing statistical and computational approaches for phylogenetic inference, trait and sequence analysis, and biodiversity quantification.
